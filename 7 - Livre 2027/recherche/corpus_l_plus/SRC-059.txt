Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is increasingly embedded in cross-border e-commerce supply chains, where digital platforms support automated content generation, product recommendation, customer interaction, supplier evaluation, compliance automation, and sustainability reporting. While these applications create opportunities for efficiency and innovation, they also introduce ethical, governance, and sustainability risks that extend beyond individual algorithms or isolated organizational processes. This study conceptualizes system-level risk as risk that emerges and propagates across interconnected data pipelines, model architectures, platform functions, organizational actors, supply-chain relationships, and cross-jurisdictional regulatory environments. Drawing on a PRISMA 2020-based systematic literature review of 66 peer-reviewed and policy-relevant publications, the article develops a structured taxonomy of GenAI-related risks and an integrated conceptual governance framework for cross-border e-commerce supply chains. The review identifies five core risk domains: bias and fairness, privacy and data governance, misinformation and manipulation, accountability and liability, and environmental sustainability. The findings suggest that these risks often interact and reinforce one another across data, model, application, organizational, and regulatory layers, rather than operating as separate technical problems. The study further maps these risk domains to governance mechanisms discussed in the literature, including explainable AI, privacy-preserving and federated learning approaches, auditability mechanisms, human-in-the-loop oversight, regulatory alignment, and green AI strategies. However, the review further indicates that these mechanisms are supported by different levels of evidence and should be understood not as universally validated solutions, but as governance enablers. The proposed framework does not seek to replace existing AI governance models, but rather complements and extends them by focusing on GenAI risk propagation in cross-border platform ecosystems, where technical design, organizational responsibility, regulatory fragmentation, and sustainability obligations intersect. The article contributes to the literature by offering a system-level synthesis of GenAI governance risks and by outlining a diagnostic framework that may support researchers, platform operators, and policymakers in assessing and governing GenAI-enabled cross-border e-commerce infrastructures. The limitations of the study include its conceptual nature, its reliance on English-language indexed literature, and the absence of primary empirical validation.

1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has significantly transformed the architecture and operation of digital platform ecosystems, particularly in cross–border e–commerce environments. By enabling automated content generation, predictive analytics, and decision support, GenAI systems are increasingly embedded within core platform functions, including product recommendation, demand forecasting, customer interaction, and compliance processes [1 ,2 ,3 ,4 ]. As a result, GenAI is no longer a peripheral analytical tool but a core component of digital platform systems, directly shaping operational processes, user interactions, and cross–border economic coordination. At the same time, the deployment of GenAI systems in cross–border digital platform environments introduces a complex set of governance, ethical, and sustainability challenges that emerge at the system level. These challenges are amplified by the distributed nature of digital platforms, the cross–jurisdictional flow of data, and the involvement of multiple actors operating under heterogeneous regulatory regimes [5 ,6 ,7 ,8 ,9 ,10 ]. Recent studies have highlighted concerns related to algorithmic bias, privacy and data governance, misinformation, accountability, and the environmental impact of large–scale AI systems [11 ,12 ,13 ,14 ,15 ]. In particular, the increasing reliance on large language models and data-intensive infrastructures raises critical questions regarding transparency, explainability, and the responsible use of AI in global digital ecosystems. Trust-related risks are also intensified by the capacity of GenAI systems to generate or amplify misleading, disinformative, or manipulative content across digital platforms [16 ]. Moreover, privacy-preserving approaches may interact with fairness outcomes in complex ways, indicating that privacy protection and bias mitigation should be understood as interconnected governance concerns in AI-enabled environments [17 ]. In addition, accountability gaps complicate the allocation of responsibility and liability when automated decisions produce social, economic, or legal consequences in multi–actor and cross–jurisdictional environments [18 ,19 ,20 ]. In this context, GenAI operates as an integral component of complex digital infrastructures, where data flows, model architectures, and platform interactions jointly shape system behavior. Beyond these concerns, the computational intensity and energy demands associated with the training and deployment of GenAI systems raise critical questions regarding environmental sustainability, including carbon footprints, Scope 3 emissions, and the alignment of AI–enabled digital infrastructures with sustainability reporting frameworks [21 ,22 ,23 ,24 ,25 ]. Existing studies predominantly examine ethical and governance issues in isolation, while the system–level interaction and propagation of these risks within cross–border digital platform environments remain insufficiently theorized and operationalized. To address this gap, the study advances a system–level analytical framework that conceptualizes how ethical, governance, and sustainability risks emerge, interact, and propagate within GenAI–enabled cross–border digital platform systems. These risks manifest across multiple operational dimensions of cross–border digital platforms, including data governance, algorithmic decision–making, platform moderation, and sustainability reporting processes.

In this study, the term system-level refers to risks and governance challenges that do not arise solely within a single algorithm, dataset, organizational process, or jurisdiction, but emerge from the interaction among data pipelines, GenAI model layers, platform functions, supply-chain actors, and heterogeneous regulatory environments. A system-level risk is therefore understood as a risk that can propagate across technical, organizational, operational, and legal layers of a cross-border digital platform ecosystem. Accordingly, this article does not propose a statistically validated risk-propagation model. Rather, it develops a structured taxonomy of GenAI-related risks and an integrated conceptual governance framework explaining how technical, organizational, and regulatory mechanisms may be aligned to identify, assess, and govern these risks in cross-border digital platform environments. Although several risks discussed in this review are also present in broader AI governance debates, GenAI-enabled cross-border platforms intensify and transform them in specific ways. Bias may affect not only predictive decisions, but also automatically generated product descriptions, recommendations, supplier evaluations, translations, and customer interactions. Privacy risks are amplified by the use of large-scale training data, inference pipelines, and cross-border data flows. Misinformation becomes more salient because GenAI systems can generate synthetic reviews, misleading product claims, hallucinated compliance information, or fraudulent marketplace content at scale. Accountability gaps are deepened by the involvement of model developers, platform operators, third-party sellers, logistics providers, and regulators operating across different jurisdictions. Environmental sustainability becomes particularly relevant because large-scale GenAI deployment increases computational demand across distributed platform infrastructures.

Against this background, and in order to clarify how GenAI-related risks emerge and propagate across cross-border digital platform environments, the study is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1: What are the main ethical, governance, and sustainability risks associated with the deployment of GenAI systems in cross-border digital platform environments?

RQ2: How do these risks emerge, interact, and propagate across AI-enabled digital platforms and supply chains?

RQ3: What governance mechanisms can support the mitigation of these risks, considering the interaction between technical architectures, organizational practices, and regulatory environments?

To answer these questions, this study conducts a PRISMA 2020–compliant systematic literature review of 66 high–quality references. The findings of the review are structured around these research questions, providing an integrated synthesis of existing knowledge and highlighting key implications for governance, policy, and future research. Beyond synthesizing the literature, the study develops an applied analytical structure that enables organizations, platform operators, and regulators to identify, prioritize, and govern ethical and sustainability risks associated with GenAI deployment in cross–border digital platform environments. The proposed framework seeks to translate abstract ethical principles into operational governance mechanisms relevant for real-world AI-enabled systems.

2 Literature background

The ethical implications of artificial intelligence have been a central topic in interdisciplinary research well before the emergence of generative models. Early contributions in AI ethics focused predominantly on fairness, transparency, and accountability, addressing risks associated with algorithmic discrimination, decision opacity, and responsibility gaps in automated systems [5 , 26 , 27 ]. These foundational studies were largely concerned with rule–based or predictive machine learning systems and emphasized normative principles, such as explainability, human oversight, and procedural justice, as prerequisites for trustworthy AI deployment. The rapid diffusion of GenAI—including large language models, generative adversarial networks, and diffusion–based architectures—has significantly expanded the ethical risk landscape. Unlike earlier AI systems, GenAI is capable of producing autonomous textual, visual, and strategic outputs, thereby introducing novel challenges related to hallucinations, misinformation, intellectual property infringement, and non–transparent reasoning processes [1 ,2 ,3 ,4 , 15 ]. Recent systematic and scoping reviews document how these risks are amplified by the scale and opacity of foundation models, whose training data, internal representations, and inference pathways remain largely inaccessible to users and regulators [1 , 2 , 4 ]. As a result, ethical concerns have shifted from isolated algorithmic decisions toward systemic risks embedded in socio–technical ecosystems. Parallel to developments in AI ethics, a distinct but increasingly interconnected body of literature has examined the application of artificial intelligence in supply chain management (SCM). Studies in this stream highlight the transformative potential of AI–driven analytics, digital twins, and predictive models for improving operational efficiency, resilience, and sustainability across complex supply networks [6 ,7 ,8 ,9 , 28 ]. In particular, recent research demonstrates how AI–enabled coordination and real–time data integration can enhance demand forecasting, inventory optimization, and disruption management in globally dispersed supply chains [6 , 7 , 9 ]. However, the SCM literature also identifies structural risks associated with advanced AI adoption. These include biased forecasting models that propagate demand distortions, failures in traceability systems that undermine product accountability, and fragmented governance arrangements that limit oversight across multi–tier and cross–border networks [29 ,30 ,31 ]. Importantly, prior contributions argue that these risks are not merely technical but are deeply embedded in organizational practices, contractual relationships, and regulatory asymmetries characterizing global supply chains [8 , 29 , 31 ]. Cross–border e–commerce supply chains represent a critical convergence point where ethical concerns related to GenAI and governance challenges in SCM intersect most acutely. Digital platforms orchestrate transactions among geographically dispersed buyers, sellers, logistics providers, and regulators, often operating across jurisdictions with heterogeneous legal, fiscal, and sustainability requirements [8 , 32 ]. In this context, GenAI is increasingly deployed to automate customer interaction, customs documentation, compliance monitoring, supplier assessment, and sustainability reporting. While such applications promise efficiency gains, they also magnify ethical risks by embedding automated decision–making into processes with direct legal, financial, and societal consequences. The governance of AI–enabled systems in cross–border e–commerce therefore extends beyond firm–level ethical guidelines or technical safeguards. It depends critically on regulatory interoperability, standardization, and cross–jurisdictional accountability mechanisms. Recent studies emphasize that fragmented regulatory environments complicate the allocation of responsibility when automated decisions lead to consumer harm, data protection violations, or environmental misreporting [18 , 33 ]. Accountability becomes particularly opaque when GenAI systems are trained, deployed, and updated across different legal regimes, involving multiple actors with overlapping yet ambiguous obligations. In response to these challenges, an emerging strand of literature advocates for integrated governance approaches that combine technical, organizational, and regulatory dimensions. However, existing studies remain divided regarding the extent to which these risks can be addressed or reduced through technical solutions alone, as several authors argue that organizational and regulatory constraints play a more decisive role. This divergence highlights the need for system–level analyses that explicitly account for the interaction between technical architectures, organizational practices, and regulatory environments. However, existing studies often underestimate the institutional and operational constraints that limit the practical implementation of governance mechanisms in cross–border platform environments. Technical measures include explainable AI, federated learning, and audit logs designed to mitigate bias, protect privacy, and enhance traceability [13 , 14 , 34 , 35 ]. Organizational mechanisms focus on human–in–the–loop controls, ethical risk assessments, and internal accountability structures aligned with supply chain governance practices [18 , 29 ]. At the regulatory level, scholars highlight the growing importance of alignment with international standards and policy frameworks addressing AI risk management, sustainability reporting, and digital market oversight [27 , 33 ]. Despite this growing body of research, the literature remains fragmented across disciplinary silos. Reviews of GenAI ethics often lack sector–specific depth, while SCM–focused studies frequently under–theorize ethical and governance implications of generative technologies. In particular, there is limited synthesis that explicitly addresses how ethical risks of GenAI manifest within cross–border e–commerce supply chains, how these risks propagate across organizational and national boundaries, and which governance mechanisms may support their identification, assessment, and mitigation. Against this backdrop, the present study contributes by systematically integrating insights from AI ethics, supply chain management, and cross–border e–commerce research. Parallel strands of the literature have focused on sector–specific applications and institutional settings, including higher education, professional practice, and research governance, revealing tensions between innovation, accountability, and ethical oversight [39 ,40 ,41 ,42 ]. The reviewed literature shows that GenAI governance remains fragmented across several research streams, including AI ethics, platform governance, supply-chain management, digital trade, and sustainability studies. While existing AI governance frameworks provide valuable general principles, they rarely explain how GenAI-related risks propagate across interconnected platform layers, supply-chain actors, and jurisdictions. This gap is particularly relevant for cross-border digital platform environments, where technical risks, organizational responsibilities, regulatory fragmentation, and sustainability obligations interact simultaneously. The present study addresses this gap by synthesizing the literature through a system-level lens and by developing a risk taxonomy and conceptual governance framework tailored to GenAI-enabled cross-border digital platform environments.

3 Methodology

3.1 Review protocol

This study adopts a systematic literature review (SLR) design and follows the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta–Analyses) reporting guidelines to ensure transparency, rigor, and replicability. The review protocol was defined ex ante, specifying the overall objectives, research questions, search strategy, eligibility criteria, and screening procedures prior to data collection. It was not registered in PROSPERO, OSF, or another public registry, as the study was designed as a single-author systematic literature review in an emerging interdisciplinary field. By adhering to PRISMA 2020, the study aims to minimize selection bias and enhance the methodological robustness of the synthesis, particularly important given the interdisciplinary nature of research on GenAI, ethics, and cross–border supply chain management. The protocol was designed to capture both conceptual and empirical contributions addressing ethical and governance risks associated with GenAI in cross–border e–commerce supply chains, while maintaining consistency with the interdisciplinary scope of the review.

3.2 Data sources and search strategy

The literature search was conducted using two major bibliographic databases: Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus. These databases were selected due to their extensive coverage of high–quality, peer–reviewed journals in the fields of artificial intelligence, information systems, supply chain management, sustainability, and electronic commerce. Search strings were constructed by combining keywords and Boolean operators related to four core dimensions: (i) GenAI (e.g., “generative AI”, “large language models”, “LLMs”, “generative models”); (ii) ethics and governance (e.g., “ethical risk”, “AI governance”, “accountability”, “bias”, “privacy”); (iii) cross–border e–commerce (e.g., “cross–border e–commerce”, “digital platforms”, “online marketplaces”); and (iv) supply chain management (e.g., “global supply chains”, “logistics”, “traceability”, “sustainability reporting”). The search was limited to publications from 2018 to 2025, reflecting the period in which GenAI technologies have gained prominence and begun to influence managerial and regulatory practices. Only documents published in English were considered, in line with common practices in systematic reviews and to ensure comparability across sources. In line with established standards for AI governance and risk management, authoritative policy and technical framework documents were also considered to contextualize academic findings [50 , 51 ].

3.3 Search strings and database fields

To improve reproducibility, the exact Boolean search strings used in Scopus and Web of Science are reported below. In Scopus, the search was conducted in the TITLE-ABS-KEY fields. In Web of Science Core Collection, the search was conducted in the Topic field, covering title, abstract, author keywords, and Keywords Plus. For Scopus, the following search string was used: TITLE-ABS-KEY (“generative AI” OR “generative artificial intelligence” OR “large language model*” OR LLM* OR “generative model*” OR “foundation model*”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“AI governance” OR “artificial intelligence governance” OR “ethical risk*” OR bias OR fairness OR privacy OR accountability OR liability OR misinformation OR manipulation OR sustainability OR “green AI”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“digital platform*” OR “online marketplace*” OR “e-commerce” OR ecommerce OR “cross-border e-commerce” OR “cross border e-commerce” OR “platform ecosystem*”) AND TITLE-ABS-KEY (“supply chain*” OR logistics OR traceability OR “data governance” OR “regulatory compliance” OR “sustainability reporting”). For Web of Science, the following search string was used: TS = (“generative AI” OR “generative artificial intelligence” OR “large language model*” OR LLM* OR “generative model*” OR “foundation model*”) AND TS = (“AI governance” OR “artificial intelligence governance” OR “ethical risk*” OR bias OR fairness OR privacy OR accountability OR liability OR misinformation OR manipulation OR sustainability OR “green AI”) AND TS = (“digital platform*” OR “online marketplace*” OR “e-commerce” OR ecommerce OR “cross-border e-commerce” OR “cross border e-commerce” OR “platform ecosystem*”) AND TS = (“supply chain*” OR logistics OR traceability OR “data governance” OR “regulatory compliance” OR “sustainability reporting”). The same time window, language restriction, and document-type eligibility criteria were applied consistently during the screening process.

3.4 Coding procedure and category assignment

After the final set of 66 studies was established, each publication was coded using a structured extraction template. Because this is a single-author study, screening, data extraction, and coding were performed by one researcher. To enhance reliability, the coding decisions were documented in an audit trail, and a subset of 13 studies, representing approximately 20% of the final corpus, was rechecked two weeks after the initial coding stage to verify consistency in category assignment, especially in cases where publications addressed more than one risk domain. The coding process recorded the publication year, methodological approach, empirical or conceptual orientation, platform or supply-chain context, main GenAI application, ethical or governance risk addressed, proposed mitigation mechanism, and system layer affected. The five risk domains were defined deductively based on the research questions and refined inductively during full-text analysis. A study was assigned to a given risk category when it explicitly discussed that risk as a substantive analytical theme rather than as a passing reference. Because several studies addressed more than one risk domain, multiple coding was allowed. Therefore, the numerical counts reported in Table 2 refer to the number of studies addressing each category and do not sum to 66.

3.5 Eligibility criteria

To ensure relevance, quality, and consistency with the objectives of this systematic review, studies were included when they met a set of predefined eligibility criteria. The review considered peer-reviewed journal articles, high-quality and high-impact conference proceedings, reputable preprint studies, including ArXiv contributions that are widely cited within the artificial intelligence, digital governance, and supply chain management literature, as well as authoritative policy documents and standard-setting frameworks relevant to AI governance and sustainability. This inclusive approach reflects established practices in AI-related systematic reviews, where cutting-edge research and governance frameworks often emerge initially in preprint repositories or leading conferences. Eligible studies were required to explicitly address GenAI or closely related generative models, such as large language models, generative adversarial networks, or diffusion-based architectures, with a clear focus on generative systems and not on artificial intelligence as a broad technological category. They also had to show direct relevance to supply chain management, electronic commerce, cross-border digital operations, or platform-based digital environments. In addition, the selected studies were expected to engage substantively with ethical, governance, accountability, regulatory, or sustainability implications associated with the adoption and use of GenAI. Studies were excluded when they were purely technical in nature, lacked a clear connection to supply chain or e-commerce contexts, or did not address ethical, governance, regulatory, accountability, or sustainability considerations. This set of eligibility criteria was applied consistently throughout the screening and full-text assessment stages to ensure methodological rigor and thematic alignment with the aims of the review.

3.6 Study selection and screening process

The initial database search yielded 210 records across Web of Science and Scopus. Following the removal of duplicate entries (n = 38), a total of 172 unique records remained for screening. These records were first screened based on titles and abstracts, resulting in the exclusion of 96 articles that did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria. The remaining 76 articles were subjected to full–text assessment to evaluate their relevance and methodological adequacy in greater depth. During this stage, 10 articles were excluded for the following reasons: insufficient linkage to cross-border e-commerce or digital platform environments (n = 4), conceptual redundancy with more comprehensive studies already included in the review (n = 3), and limited discussion of ethical, governance, regulatory, accountability, or sustainability implications (n = 3). As a result, 66 references were retained and included in the final synthesis. These studies constitute the analytical foundation of the review and are fully reflected in the reference list of the article. Taken together, Table 3 illustrates how ethical risk categories identified in the literature translate into concrete governance challenges and managerial responses across cross–border e–commerce supply chain functions. Bias and fairness emerge as the most frequently discussed category, addressed in 24 studies. Complementing these findings, recent studies on GenAI adoption across business sectors and digitally transformed supply chains emphasize that ethical risk management in practice is closely intertwined with organizational implementation challenges, digital transformation trajectories, and sustainability–oriented operational redesign, particularly in complex, platform–mediated environments [53 ,54 ,55 ]. The literature documents how biased training data and model architectures can distort demand forecasting, dynamic pricing, supplier evaluation, and credit scoring in digital marketplaces [5 , 10 , 11 , 56 ]. In cross–border contexts, such biases risk reinforcing structural inequalities between regions and market participants, thereby undermining fairness and competitive neutrality. Privacy and data governance constitute the second most prominent category, appearing in 20 studies. Scholars emphasize the heightened sensitivity of personal, transactional, and behavioral data in cross–border e–commerce, where GenAI systems often process information subject to divergent data protection regimes [12 ,13 ,14 , 35 , 57 ]. Recent research also suggests that privacy-preserving approaches may interact with fairness outcomes in complex ways, indicating that privacy protection and bias mitigation should be treated as interconnected governance objectives rather than as fully separate technical concerns [17 ]. Misinformation and manipulation, discussed in 15 studies, represent a distinctive challenge associated with generative models. Hallucinated outputs, synthetic reviews, deepfakes, and automated content generation threaten trust in online marketplaces and platform governance mechanisms [15 , 16 , 19 , 20 ]. Several studies warn that such risks are amplified in cross–border e–commerce, where verification mechanisms and legal remedies are unevenly enforced. Accountability and liability issues are identified in 12 studies, reflecting growing concern over opaque decision–making and unclear responsibility allocation in GenAI–enabled systems [18 , 33 ]. The literature highlights difficulties in attributing accountability when automated decisions involve multiple actors, including platform operators, model developers, third–party vendors, and logistics partners operating across jurisdictions. Lastly, environmental sustainability emerges as a significant but comparatively underexplored category, addressed in 11 studies. Although environmental sustainability is the least frequent category in the reviewed dataset, its governance relevance should not be underestimated. The lower number of studies indicates an underdeveloped research area, while its practical relevance for GenAI governance remains substantial. These contributions focus on the energy intensity of GenAI training and inference, the carbon footprint of data centers, and the implications for Scope 3 emissions reporting within global supply chains [21 ,22 ,23 ,24 ,25 ]. In cross-border digital platform environments, GenAI-related energy consumption and carbon emissions may become increasingly important as platforms scale automated content generation, recommendation systems, customer interaction tools, compliance automation, and sustainability reporting. These impacts are also relevant for ESG disclosure and the broader assessment of digital infrastructures within sustainable supply chains. Several authors argue that sustainability risks associated with GenAI challenge prevailing assumptions about digitalization as an inherently “green” transformation.

4.3 Cross–border e–commerce and supply chain implications

Beyond categorizing ethical risks, the reviewed studies provide insights into how these risks manifest and propagate across cross–border e–commerce supply chain functions. GenAI applications such as automated customs documentation, predictive logistics, supplier screening, and sustainability reporting promise efficiency gains but also embed ethical risks directly into operational decision–making [6 , 8 , 9 ]. Bias and misinformation can cascade through interconnected supply chain processes, affecting procurement decisions, inventory allocation, and fraud detection [29 , 31 ]. Privacy and data governance risks intensify when GenAI systems integrate data from multiple jurisdictions, increasing exposure to regulatory sanctions and reputational damage [13 , 14 ]. Accountability gaps become particularly salient in platform–based ecosystems, where responsibility is distributed among multiple actors with limited contractual transparency [18 , 20 , 33 ]. These findings reinforce the view that ethical risks associated with GenAI in cross-border e-commerce supply chains are systemic, not isolated, and therefore require governance approaches that extend beyond technical fixes. The concentration of multiple risk categories within single operational contexts underscores the need for integrated frameworks capable of addressing ethical, regulatory, and sustainability concerns simultaneously.

5 Discussion

5.1 Interpretation of ethical and governance risks

The findings of this systematic review indicate that ethical risks associated with GenAI-enabled cross-border e-commerce supply chains emerge as systemic concerns shaped by the interaction between advanced generative technologies, complex organizational structures, and fragmented regulatory environments. As evidenced in Table 2 , multiple ethical risk categories—bias, privacy, misinformation, accountability, and sustainability—frequently co–occur within the same operational contexts, reinforcing each other and amplifying their overall impact. One key insight concerns the cascading nature of bias–related risks. Prior contributions show that biased training data or model outputs can propagate across interconnected supply chain processes, affecting demand forecasting, supplier selection, and fraud detection simultaneously [5 , 10 , 11 , 29 , 31 ]. In cross–border e–commerce, such cascades are particularly problematic because algorithmic decisions often scale rapidly across markets, thereby reinforcing structural asymmetries between regions and market participants. Such dynamics reinforce calls for modeling ethical risk propagation across multi–tier supply chains and platform ecosystems, particularly in the context of cross–border digital trade [58 ]. These findings extend earlier work on algorithmic fairness by highlighting how bias in GenAI systems may evolve from a localized technical issue into a systemic governance challenge [5 , 26 , 27 ]. Privacy and data governance risks further underscore the vulnerability of cross–border supply chains to ethical failures. As documented in multiple studies [12 ,13 ,14 , 35 , 59 , 60 ], GenAI systems routinely process personal, transactional, and behavioral data originating from jurisdictions with divergent data protection regimes. Privacy breaches or non-compliant data flows expose firms to regulatory sanctions and may also erode trust among consumers, partners, and regulators. The literature reviewed suggests that privacy risks are closely intertwined with accountability gaps, particularly when responsibility for data processing is distributed among platform operators, model developers, and third–party service providers [12 ,13 ,14 , 17 , 18 ]. The review also highlights the growing relevance of misinformation and manipulation risks associated with generative models. Hallucinated outputs, automated fake reviews, and synthetic content can undermine trust in digital marketplaces and compromise platform governance mechanisms [15 , 16 , 19 , 20 ]. In cross–border settings, the impact of such risks is magnified by differences in enforcement capacity and legal remedies, making it difficult to address reputational damage or consumer harm once misinformation has propagated across–borders. From a technical risk perspective, authoritative analyses of adversarial machine learning further document how generative models are vulnerable to manipulation through evasion attacks, data poisoning, and model exploitation techniques, which can facilitate the large–scale generation and dissemination of misleading or harmful content across digital platforms [60 ]. A particularly salient finding concerns environmental sustainability, which, although less frequently addressed than other risk categories, represents a critical emerging governance challenge. Studies focusing on the carbon footprint of GenAI training and inference question the assumption that digitalization inherently supports sustainability objectives [21 ,22 ,23 ,24 ,25 ]. In cross–border supply chains, these concerns intersect with expanding requirements for Scope 3 emissions reporting and sustainability disclosures, raising questions about the accountability of firms deploying energy–intensive GenAI systems across global networks. Beyond operational efficiency, recent research increasingly links GenAI deployment to broader ESG reporting, carbon accounting practices, green supply chain innovation, and the environmental performance of digital infrastructures [59 ,60 ,61 ,62 ,63 ,64 ]. These sustainability concerns also intersect with accountability debates, particularly when responsibility for AI-related environmental impacts must be allocated across developers, platform operators, data infrastructure providers, and supply-chain actors [65 , 66 ]. As shown in the results, environmental sustainability remains underdeveloped in the reviewed literature despite its growing relevance for Scope 3 reporting, ESG disclosure, and the governance of large-scale GenAI infrastructures. In response to these interconnected risks, the literature increasingly points to the relevance of hybrid governance approaches. Rather than relying solely on technical fixes or regulatory compliance, scholars advocate integrated solutions combining technical safeguards, organizational controls, and regulatory alignment [13 , 14 , 18 , 34 , 35 ]. Technical measures such as federated learning, explainable AI, and auditability mechanisms aim to mitigate bias, protect privacy, and enhance transparency. In this regard, standard–setting work on adversarial machine learning emphasizes that mitigation of ethical and operational risks may require embedding security–aware design principles, robustness testing, and continuous monitoring mechanisms into the lifecycle governance of GenAI systems [60 ]. Organizational responses include human–in–the–loop controls, ethical risk assessments, and supply chain governance structures that clarify responsibility across actors. At the regulatory level, alignment with emerging AI governance frameworks and sustainability standards is presented as an important condition for managing ethical risks in cross-border contexts. These governance challenges unfold within broader trade and environmental policy frameworks, where evolving digital trade agreements, sustainability–related border measures, and environmental disclosure requirements increasingly shape the design and operation of cross–border e–commerce supply chains. The reviewed literature supports these governance mechanisms with different degrees of evidentiary strength. Mechanisms such as audit logs, human oversight, and regulatory alignment are widely supported as general governance requirements, but their effectiveness depends on institutional capacity, organizational implementation, and enforcement conditions. Federated learning and privacy-preserving computation are technically promising for reducing data-transfer risks, yet their implementation in cross-border platform environments remains uneven and context-dependent. Explainability can improve transparency and contestability, but it does not automatically guarantee fairness or accountability. Green AI strategies and carbon-aware computing are increasingly discussed in conceptual and technical literature, but empirical evidence on their large-scale effectiveness in platform-based GenAI deployments remains limited. Therefore, these mechanisms should be understood not as universally validated solutions, but as governance enablers.

The review indicates that the governance of AI-enabled systems in cross-border digital platform environments should be understood as a multi-layered and system-dependent process. Governance does not depend only on isolated technical fixes or formal compliance measures, but on the interaction between data infrastructures, model architectures, organizational practices, and regulatory frameworks operating across jurisdictions. 5.3 Framework originality and relation to existing AI governance models

The framework proposed in this study does not seek to replace established AI governance models such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Its contribution lies in adapting and extending general AI governance principles to the specific context of GenAI-enabled cross-border digital platform environments. Its originality derives from conceptualizing GenAI risks as system-level risks that may propagate across data, model, platform, organizational, and regulatory layers. It also links ethical and governance risks to concrete platform and supply-chain functions, including supplier evaluation, marketplace trust, logistics documentation, compliance automation, and sustainability reporting. In addition, cross-jurisdictional regulatory fragmentation and environmental sustainability are treated as structural governance conditions, not as external or secondary considerations. The framework therefore provides a context-specific conceptualization of AI governance for digital platforms operating across borders.

5.4 Illustrative application to a cross-border marketplace

To illustrate the practical relevance of the proposed framework, consider a cross-border online marketplace that uses GenAI to generate product descriptions, translate seller information, support customer service chatbots, detect fraudulent listings, recommend products, evaluate sellers, and prepare sustainability-related disclosures. In such a setting, bias and fairness risks may arise if product recommendations or seller rankings systematically disadvantage suppliers from certain regions, languages, or market segments. Privacy and data governance risks may occur when customer, seller, and logistics data are processed across jurisdictions with different data protection requirements. Misinformation and manipulation risks may emerge through hallucinated product claims, synthetic reviews, misleading translations, or automatically generated compliance statements. Accountability and liability risks become salient when responsibility for erroneous outputs is distributed among the model provider, platform operator, third-party seller, and logistics partner. Environmental sustainability risks arise from the computational resources required to operate GenAI tools at scale and from the difficulty of allocating emissions across platform services and supply-chain actors. Applying the proposed framework, the platform would need to combine technical safeguards, such as bias testing, explainability interfaces, content verification, privacy-preserving architectures, and audit logs, with organizational controls, such as human review for high-risk decisions, seller accountability procedures, and escalation mechanisms. These measures would also need to be aligned with cross-border regulatory requirements concerning data protection, consumer protection, platform accountability, and sustainability reporting. This illustrative application shows how the framework may be used to diagnose risk propagation and identify governance responses across interconnected platform layers.

5.5 Research gaps and structured research agenda

Building on the results of this review, this section identifies key structural research gaps and proposes a structured research agenda for advancing the governance of GenAI in cross–border e–commerce supply chains. The literature remains fragmented with respect to standardized approaches for assessing ethical risks, particularly in operational supply chain settings where multiple risks may co–occur and propagate across organizational and jurisdictional boundaries. In addition, empirical evidence on how governance mechanisms function across heterogeneous regulatory regimes is still limited, leaving open questions regarding accountability allocation, regulatory interoperability, and enforcement effectiveness in cross–border contexts. Organizational capabilities for implementing human–in–the–loop governance are frequently advocated in principle, yet remain insufficiently examined in practice, while the environmental and Scope 3 implications of GenAI are underrepresented relative to their growing relevance for corporate sustainability and reporting obligations. 6 Conclusions

This article examined the governance of generative artificial intelligence risks in cross-border digital platform environments through a PRISMA 2020-compliant systematic literature review of 66 peer-reviewed and policy-relevant publications. The review identified five recurring risk domains: bias and fairness, privacy and data governance, misinformation and manipulation, accountability and liability, and environmental sustainability. These domains show how GenAI-related risks are discussed across the intersecting literatures on AI ethics, digital platforms, supply-chain management, regulatory governance, and sustainability. The findings indicate that GenAI-related risks are not limited to isolated technical components, individual datasets, or single platform functions. They may emerge and interact across data pipelines, model architectures, platform applications, organizational processes, supply-chain relationships, and cross-jurisdictional regulatory environments. The system-level perspective developed in this article therefore emphasizes the propagation of ethical, governance, and sustainability concerns across technical, operational, organizational, and legal layers of cross-border digital platform ecosystems. The article contributes by developing a structured taxonomy of GenAI-related risks and by proposing a conceptual governance framework that links risk domains, platform and supply-chain functions, governance mechanisms, and cross-border regulatory conditions. This framework does not replace established AI governance models, but adapts their logic to the specific context of GenAI-enabled cross-border digital platform environments. It also provides a diagnostic perspective for examining how risks may propagate across data, model, platform, organizational, and regulatory layers. The review further suggests that governance responses to GenAI-related risks require alignment between technical safeguards, organizational controls, regulatory interoperability, and sustainability-oriented reporting obligations. Mechanisms such as explainability, privacy-preserving architectures, federated learning, auditability, human-in-the-loop oversight, content verification, regulatory alignment, and green AI strategies may support risk mitigation when embedded in broader governance arrangements. Their effectiveness, however, depends on the architecture of the platform, organizational capacity, enforcement conditions, and the specific cross-border context in which GenAI systems are deployed. The illustrative application to a cross-border marketplace shows how the proposed framework may be used to interpret risk interactions in a realistic platform setting. GenAI-generated product descriptions, automated translations, seller evaluations, fraud detection tools, customer service chatbots, and sustainability-related disclosures may simultaneously raise concerns related to bias, privacy, misinformation, accountability, and environmental impact. This illustration clarifies the analytical usefulness of the framework, while the empirical testing and refinement of the framework remain tasks for future research. Environmental sustainability also emerges as an increasingly relevant dimension of GenAI governance. Although it appears less frequently in the reviewed literature than other risk domains, its importance is likely to grow as platforms expand GenAI-based infrastructures and services. Energy consumption, carbon footprint, Scope 3 reporting, ESG disclosure, and emissions allocation across platform and supply-chain actors should therefore be treated as integral components of system-level governance rather than as peripheral sustainability issues.

6.1 Limitations and future research

The findings should be interpreted in light of several methodological and conceptual constraints. Although the review follows PRISMA 2020 principles, it is limited to publications indexed in Scopus and Web of Science and to English-language sources, which may exclude relevant regional, legal, or policy contributions. The field of GenAI governance is also evolving rapidly, and some recent technical or regulatory developments may not yet be fully reflected in the academic literature. In addition, the coding of risk domains involved interpretive judgment, particularly because many studies addressed multiple risks simultaneously. The single-coder design should also be acknowledged as a methodological limitation, although consistency was supported through a documented coding trail and repeated checking of selected records. The proposed framework is conceptual and has not been statistically tested or validated through primary empirical data. It should therefore be understood as an analytical and diagnostic tool rather than as a predictive model. Another limitation concerns the uneven evidentiary basis of the governance mechanisms discussed in the article, since some are grounded in empirical studies, while others remain primarily conceptual, technical, or policy-oriented proposals. Future research should test and refine the framework through case studies, longitudinal research, expert validation, and comparative analyses across platform types and jurisdictions. Particular attention should be given to the empirical performance of explainability, auditability, human oversight, federated learning, privacy-preserving computation, content verification, and green AI strategies in operational GenAI systems. Further research should also develop measurable indicators for system-level risk propagation and examine how environmental impacts of GenAI can be incorporated into ESG and Scope 3 reporting frameworks. Comparative studies across regulatory regimes would be particularly useful for assessing how data protection rules, platform accountability requirements, consumer protection standards, and sustainability reporting obligations shape the practical governance of GenAI-enabled cross-border digital platforms.
