Abstract
The rapid proliferation of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies has transformed work
practices and skill requirements across industries, particularly within the Information
Technology (IT) sector. Unlike traditional automation tools, agentic AI possesses autonomous
decision-making capabilities and can collaborate with humans as “team members” in complex
software development tasks. While existing research has examined Agentic AI's productivity
impacts in software development, there remains a limited understanding of how it influences
skill changes across different experience levels. This article aims to investigate whether the
collaboration between agentic agents and human agents influences the developers’ skills in the
software industry. To achieve this aim, we conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) that
examines the impact of agentic AI on developer skills in the software industry. The findings
indicate that agentic AI does not remove the need for developer skills but instead reshapes them.
As AI agents take on routine and repetitive development tasks, human developers increasingly
focus on higher-level activities such as system design, coordination, supervision, and
governance. The outcome of this literature review will allow us to initiate further research for
understanding how agentic AI impacts software development skills.

1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence has become increasingly embedded in software development work.
Recent advances in machine learning and large language models have transformed AI from a
simple decision-support tool to one that requires combining AI and human expertise more
collaboratively (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025).
While earlier forms of AI were largely decision-support or automation tools (Davenport &
Ronanki, 2018), recent developments have led to the emergence of agentic AI. AI agents are
characterized by autonomy, goal-directed behavior, and the ability to act on behalf of humans
(Wooldridge & Jennings, 1998). These systems not only provide recommendations but can also
plan tasks, make decisions, coordinate actions, and execute workflows with minimal human
intervention (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Seeber et al., 2020). They interpret intent, manage
complex sub-goals, and operate with a degree of independence. So, agentic AI is intelligent
systems that exhibit autonomy, learning, and proactivity, and act as partners or agents on behalf
of human professionals (Benbya et al., 2020).
In the software industry, agentic AI is being targeted for activities such as code generation,
testing, debugging, and task coordination (Li et al., 2024) that were previously considered
uniquely human domains. As a result, the role and the skill requirements of the software
developer are changing. Skills in the agentic AI paradigm refer to higher-order human
capabilities centered on orchestration, judgment, problem framing, learning stewardship, and
governance that enable effective collaboration with autonomous, goal-directed AI systems
rather than direct task execution (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Faraj et al., 2018; Benbya et al.,
2020; Jarrahi, 2018; Ågerfalk, 2020). This shift raises important questions about how developer
skills are affected, how skills evolve, and whether different experience levels (such as early-
career versus experienced developers) are impacted differently.
In software development specifically, this is becoming relevant as agentic AI tools (e.g., code
assistants and multi-step coding agents) propose, generate, test, and refine code, moving beyond
static recommendations to more interactive human-AI collaboration cycles (Peng et al., 2023;
Cui et al., 2024; Campero et al., 2022). Although practitioner-oriented discussions emphasize
productivity improvements (Peng et al., 2023; Vaithilingam et al., 2022), academic research-
particularly in Information Systems Management has begun to examine deeper implications
related to skills. For example, Baird & Maruping (2021) explain the delegation hand-off
between human-AI systems, while Faraj et al. (2018) argue that learning algorithms shift human
work away from execution toward interpretation and judgment. Benbya et al. (2020) frame AI
systems as organizational actors with agency, requiring new governance and stewardship skills.
Raisch & Krakowski (2021) explain skill polarization, reskilling, and the rise of meta-skills
(problem framing, orchestration, judgment). Mikalef et al. (2019) discuss technical, analytical,
and human skills required to exploit AI systems. To the best of our knowledge, research studies
on human–agentic AI collaboration in software work are still emerging, so most insights come

2. Definition of Concepts
2.1. Defining Agentic AI
Agentic AI can be understood as a class of intelligent systems that act with a degree of
autonomy on behalf of human professionals. These systems are capable of setting and pursuing
goals, learning from their environment, and carrying out complex, multi-step tasks with limited
human oversight (Baird & Maruping, 2021; Benbya et al., 2020; Wooldridge & Jennings, 1998).
What sets agentic AI apart from conventional tools is the nature of the interaction: rather than
responding to step-by-step instructions, an agentic system is given a broad objective and works
out on its own how to accomplish it - breaking the objective down into smaller tasks, deciding
on the order of actions, and adjusting its course when things do not go as expected (Baird &
Maruping, 2021). In short, a traditional tool is operated, whereas an agentic system is delegated
to.
It is worth noting that agentic AI is sometimes confused with two related but distinct categories
of technology. The first is LLM-based generative AI, including the emerging practice of "vibe
coding," where developers describe what they want in plain language and accept AI-generated
code with little scrutiny. While useful, these systems are fundamentally reactive - they respond
to prompts one at a time and do not retain context across tasks. The second is low-code and no-
code platforms, which simplify software creation through visual interfaces and pre-built
components but do not reason, learn, or pursue goals on their own. Agentic AI tools (Appendix
A) differ from both in that it maintains an ongoing awareness of the task at hand, plans ahead
across several steps, and takes an active part in problem-solving and decision-making.
Tarafdar et al. (2022) further conceptualize algorithms as co-workers and examine role
interactions in algorithmic work. They identify several interaction patterns: humans relying on
algorithmic outputs, humans modifying algorithmic outputs, algorithms learning from human
inputs, and reciprocal adaptation where both human and algorithm adjust to each other. These
interaction patterns become especially relevant in the agentic paradigm, where the degree of
mutual adaptation between human and AI is substantially higher than in traditional tool-use
relationships.

2.2. Skill and Changing Skills
In the context of technology, Leonardi (2011) explains skills as the ability to effectively engage
with material and digital artifacts to accomplish work goals, emphasizing that skills change as
technologies change. Classic task-reallocation research shows new technologies displace some
tasks while creating others, changing the skill content of jobs (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2019;
Autor et al., 2003). More recently, Leonardi & Barley (2024) describe skills as part of evolving
expertise, where professionals must continuously adapt their knowledge and judgment as
technologies increasingly participate in decision-making.
In the context of agentic AI, the skills that matter most are no longer about carrying out tasks
directly. Instead, they involve higher-order capabilities such as orchestrating AI workflows,
exercising judgment over AI outputs, framing problems in ways that AI systems can act on,
guiding how learning takes place within human-AI teams, and governing the boundaries of AI
autonomy. These are the competencies that allow professionals to work effectively alongside
AI systems that operate with a degree of independence and pursue goals on their own (Raisch
& Krakowski, 2021; Faraj et al., 2018; Jarrahi, 2018).
Together, these developments underline the need for a systematic literature review to identify
and synthesize how agentic AI is reshaping software developers’ skills.
3. Literature Review Methodology
This study adopted a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) methodology guided by well-
established methodological recommendations in the Information Systems field, particularly
those proposed by Webster & Watson (2002) and further supported by later IS review practices
(Bandara et al., 2015).
3.1. Research Question
The research question guiding this literature review is: How does agentic AI affect the skills,
skill development, and work roles of software developers in the software industry?

3.3. Scope and Eligibility Definition
The scope of the review was defined using explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, as
recommended for systematic reviews (Webster & Watson, 2002).
3.3.1. Primary Evidence
The review prioritized peer-reviewed journal articles published between January 2018 and
October 2025. To ensure conceptual relevance, the review strictly included studies that
explicitly examined agentic AI, AI agents, or autonomous agents, and that addressed issues
related to skills, expertise, delegation, or role changes in work contexts relevant to software
development. Studies that discussed artificial intelligence without agentic behavior, conference
papers, practitioner reports, or research outside Information Systems and Management domains
were excluded. Research that did not address skills or work-related outcomes was also
excluded. This primary corpus constitutes the formal SLR sample and forms the basis of the
thematic findings presented in Section 4.
3.3.2. Complementary Evidence
Agentic AI technologies are evolving at a pace that outstrips the traditional academic
publication cycle. As a result, some relevant empirical evidence on AI-assisted software
development currently exists in the form of working papers issued by well-regarded research

small number of empirical working papers were included in the review as a separate,

3.4. Journal and Database Selection
In line with quality-focused review practices in IS research, the review prioritized journals
ranked A or A* in the ABDC Journal Quality List (Webster & Watson, 2002; Bandara et al.,
2015). The primary focus was on leading IS and Management journals, including MIS
Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems,
Information and Organization, Organization Science, and Academy of Management Journal.
Where ABDC-ranked journals provided limited coverage of agentic AI, top-quartile (Q1)
Scimago journals were included selectively to avoid omitting relevant high-quality research.
Searches were conducted using Scopus, Web of Science, and publisher-specific platforms. In
addition, targeted searches were conducted directly on journal websites to ensure coverage of
recent publications.

We intentionally included the broad term "artificial intelligence" in our search alongside
specific terms like "agentic AI." This decision was driven by two factors. First, the Information
Systems field has not yet established a clear boundary between agentic AI and general AI
(Ågerfalk, 2020; Berente et al., 2021; Baird & Maruping, 2021). Many studies examine agentic
behaviors-such as autonomous decision-making or proactive collaboration-but simply refer to
the technology as "artificial intelligence" (e.g., Jarrahi, 2018; Faraj et al., 2018; Kellogg et al.,
2020; Raisch & Krakowski, 2021). Using only narrow keywords would have excluded these
conceptually relevant papers.
Second, the specific term "agentic AI" is still emerging in the literature. An initial search using
only that phrase and its direct synonyms returned very few results. To address this, we cast a
wider net with our search terms but applied screening criteria during the reading phase. This
strategy ensured we gathered enough literature for a meaningful analysis while strictly limiting
our final sample to studies that actually focus on agentic behaviors and skill transformations.
After finalizing the search expressions, searches were applied to article titles, abstracts, and
keywords.

3.8. Thematic Analysis
Given the conceptual and methodological diversity of the selected studies, a thematic synthesis
approach (Braun & Clarke, 2006) was used. Consistent with established guidelines for
systematic literature reviews in information systems research (Okoli & Schabram, 2010),
themes were iteratively developed through abstraction, allowing for theory-oriented synthesis
rather than descriptive summarization. This approach has been widely adopted in structured
literature reviews (Thomas & Harden, 2008).
The analysis followed a three-stage coding procedure:
Stage 1- Initial coding: Each of the 15 peer-reviewed articles was read in full and annotated
with descriptive codes capturing key concepts, mechanisms, and findings. Codes were
generated inductively from the data rather than imposed a priori. For example, reading Baird &
Maruping (2021) produced codes such as "AI agency," "delegated authority," and "agentic IS
artifacts." Reading Dennis et al. (2023) yielded "AI as team member," "trust," "perceived ability
and integrity," and "opportunity vs. threat framing." In total, the initial coding pass produced
56 descriptive codes across the 15 articles.
Stage 2 - Category formation: Initial codes were then grouped into higher-level categories
based on conceptual similarity. For instance, codes such as "AI agency," "delegated authority,"
"bidirectional delegation," and "agentic IS artifacts" (from Baird & Maruping, 2021) together
with "cobots," "delegation strategies," and "AI proficiency effects" (from Sowa et al., 2021)
and "human-AI symbiosis" and "contextual understanding vs. data processing" (from Jarrahi,
2018) were grouped under a category, “Delegation and Skill Transformation”. This process
yielded eight intermediate categories.
Stage 3- Theme abstraction: The eight categories were further abstracted into four
overarching themes through iterative comparison and refinement, consistent with established
guidelines for systematic literature reviews in information systems research (Okoli &
Schabram, 2010). The four themes are: (1) Delegation and Skill Re-composition; (2) Shift from
Coding to Orchestration Skills; (3) Human-AI Teaming; (4) Differential Skill Impacts Across
Experience Levels. Table 3 presents the concept-coding matrix that maps the 15 peer-reviewed

4. Findings
The analysis organized findings into four interconnected themes that capture the transformation
of skills relevant to the concept of agency in AI. The findings presented below are derived
exclusively from the 15 peer-reviewed articles that constitute the primary evidence.
Complementary evidence from the empirical working papers is examined separately in Section
5.2.
4.1. Delegation to Agentic AI and Skill Re-composition
A central theme across the literature is the delegation of tasks and decision-making authority
from humans to agentic AI systems. The emerging paradigm is that of AI Agency, which can
"act with delegated authority to affect change in the world" (Baird & Maruping, 2021). This
distinction – from a tool to “agentic IS artifacts” (Baird & Maruping, 2021) – is important: a
tool is operated, but an agent is delegated to. Dennis et al. (2023) found that AI team members
can take on tasks independently and redistribute work to humans or other AI agents.
This shift does not imply that human skills become less important. Instead, skills are
recomposed. Technical skills remain necessary, but they are complemented by higher-level
cognitive and supervisory capabilities. Jarrahi (2018) proposed a symbiotic view where AI
handles data processing and pattern recognition while humans contribute contextual
understanding and intuitive judgment. This division requires workers to strengthen distinctly
human capabilities-such as emotional intelligence, ethical reasoning, and creative synthesis-
while developing new skills in AI collaboration.
4.2. Shift from Coding to Orchestration Skills
This theme explores how employees’ skills evolve as new technologies -especially AI-
transform their tasks and responsibilities. The peer-reviewed literature shows that generative

and agentic AI accelerate a shift away from routine execution while amplifying higher-order
reasoning.
Noy & Zhang (2023) demonstrated that AI assistance improved productivity across multiple
dimensions, with effects varying by task complexity and individual characteristics. Importantly,
the gains were not uniform: workers facing simpler, more structured tasks benefited more
immediately, while complex tasks required workers to orchestrate AI outputs more actively -
suggesting that orchestration skills become increasingly important as task difficulty rises.
Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) found that generative AI enabled customer service representatives to
resolve 14% more issues per hour. The technology effectively transferred knowledge from
experienced workers to novices, reducing the time needed to achieve competency. This suggests
that orchestration skills-knowing how to leverage AI to access organizational expertise-become
more valuable than accumulated individual experience.
Chen & Chan (2024) challenge the traditional assumption that humans provide "usefulness"
while AI provides "novelty". Instead, it suggests that AI as a co-creation partner can
significantly enhance both the perceived novelty and usefulness of outputs.
Experimental research by Dennis et al. (2023) shows that when AI agents are introduced as
team members, human participants adjust their behavior by focusing more on coordination,
conflict resolution, and decision-making rather than execution tasks. Kellogg et al. (2020)
suggest that the value of experts shifts from hands-on work to orchestration when AI is
competent enough to automate parts of tasks.
Freshers often experience rapid upskilling when AI provides examples and structured feedback,
but they also risk shallow learning if they depend too heavily on AI outputs (Raisch &
Krakowski, 2021).
4.3. Human-AI Teaming
Human–AI Teaming is based on the idea that AI systems are no longer just tools but teammates
that share tasks, goals, and responsibilities with humans. Human–AI teaming is seen as a form
of collective intelligence, where humans provide context, ethics, and creativity, while AI
contributes speed, pattern recognition, and autonomous problem solving (Faraj et al., 2018).
Dellermann et al. (2019) explored the concept of hybrid intelligence, which argues that humans
and AI contribute different strengths and must be intentionally integrated. Later on, Seeber et
al. (2020) called for systematic research on how collaboration researchers could study the
design of machine teammates and their effects on work practices and outcomes in team
collaboration. They emphasized the need to understand how AI team members affect human
team members, collaborative work practices, and organizational outcomes.
Dennis et al. (2023) provided initial empirical evidence that AI team members are perceived as
having greater ability and integrity but lower benevolence; perceiving AI as an opportunity
fostered positive outcomes, while threat perceptions engendered negative results. This suggests
that the framing of AI's role - as a collaborator rather than a replacement - significantly shapes

the quality of human-AI teaming and the willingness of developers to engage in productive

Nguyen & Elbanna (2025) identified several key themes relevant to software development.
First, AI is shifting emphasis from routine technical tasks to higher-order cognitive activities
like strategic planning, system design, and problem-solving. Second, effective AI collaboration
requires new competencies, including AI literacy, critical evaluation of AI outputs, and the
ability to guide AI systems effectively. Third, organizations need to support this transition
through training, clear role definitions, and frameworks for human-AI collaboration.
4.4. Differential Skill Impacts Across Experience Levels
The peer-reviewed literature suggests that the impact of AI agency on skills differs depending
on their experience and skill levels. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) documented that the largest
productivity gains from generative AI occurred among novice and low-skilled customer service
workers. This suggests AI may have equalizing effects, helping those with less experience or
more time pressure achieve performance closer to their more advantaged peers.
Chen & Chan (2024) provide complementary evidence from creative work: less-expert users
benefit most from AI-lead collaboration modes, while experts benefit more from co-creation
modes. This indicates that the optimal collaboration pattern itself is experience-dependent,
which has direct implications for how software organizations should design AI-assisted
workflows for developers at different career stages.
Lu et al. (2024) demonstrate that user proficiency interacts with AI capability, so that
performance improves most when users have enough skill to interpret and monitor AI outputs.
Xu et al. (2025) show that AI proficiency itself is an important predictor of collaboration
success. Users with higher AI proficiency showed better task performance and increased trust
in AI agents. These findings support a focus on freshers and experienced workers as distinct
groups in this study.
Kellogg et al. (2020) suggest that the value of experts shifts from hands-on work to
orchestration when AI is competent enough to automate parts of tasks. Sowa et al. (2021) found
that differences in AI proficiency affect delegation strategies and the degree of effective
collaboration within teams. They emphasized that boundaries, factors, and circumstances of
human-AI collaboration should be studied empirically to understand how worker characteristics
influence outcomes.
Nguyen & Elbanna (2025) reinforce that demographic characteristics and professional
background create heterogeneity in collaboration outcomes. Raisch & Krakowski (2021) warn
that this experience-dependent dynamic creates an automation-augmentation paradox: the very
automation that accelerates novice productivity may undermine the deep practice that would
develop genuine expertise, while experienced workers risk skill atrophy if they delegate
excessively.

5. Discussion
5.1. Synthesis of Systematic Literature Review Findings
The fifteen peer-reviewed studies collectively reveal that AI adoption in organizations creates
complex dynamics across all four thematic areas. The shift toward delegation to agentic AI
demands fundamental skill re-composition, as workers transition from hands-on execution to
strategic orchestration of AI capabilities. Technical skills remain important, but they are
increasingly combined with meta-skills such as oversight, governance, and coordination.
The emergence of genuine human-AI teaming requires new frameworks for collaboration that
treat AI as an active partner rather than a passive tool. Throughout these transformations,
impacts vary significantly across experience levels, with less experienced workers often gaining
the most from AI support but also facing unique challenges in developing appropriate trust and
evaluation skills.
For early-career developers, structured learning environments are necessary to prevent shallow
skill development. For experienced developers, continuous reskilling is required to adapt to
supervisory and governance roles.

These four studies (Table 4) broadly reinforce the SLR findings:

On orchestration skills (Theme 2): Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) document two distinct

consultants below average performance increased their output by 43% when using AI, while
that above average improved by 17%. This is the most precise quantification available of the
differential AI impact that is theorized but not precisely measured in the peer-reviewed SLR
corpus. Peng et al. (2023) similarly show that GitHub Copilot disproportionately benefits less-
experienced developers. Cui et al. (2025) confirm that less experienced developers had higher
adoption rates and greater productivity gains.
5.4. Limitations
Despite these convergences, these studies also highlight some limitations that must be noted:
From productivity to skill: A critical tension exists between what the working papers measure
and what this review seeks to understand. Cui et al. (2025), Dell'Acqua et al. (2023), and Peng
et al. (2023) primarily measure productivity outcomes (task completion rates, speed, output
quality). The peer-reviewed literature (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Faraj et al., 2018) warns
that productivity gains do not automatically translate into skill development.
Generalizability: The working papers report on specific organizational settings (BCG,
Microsoft, Accenture) and specific AI tools (GitHub Copilot, GPT-4). Whether these findings
transfer to the full diversity of software development contexts (e.g., startups, open-source
projects, safety-critical systems) remains to be established through peer-reviewed replications.
While these working papers originate from respected institutions and employ rigorous
methodologies, they have not undergone formal peer review. Their findings should be
interpreted with the understanding that they may be revised through the review process.

5.5.1. Lack of Software-Industry-Specific Evidence
Most of the peer-reviewed literature available discusses Human-AI collaboration broadly
(Raisch & Krakowski, 2021; Faraj et al., 2018), but Agentic AI - AI systems with autonomy,
proactivity, and delegated authority - is not well-studied in the software development context.
This suggests that most empirical work is on generative AI assistants (e.g., Copilot, GPT) and
general human–AI collaboration, but not on agentic AI. Hence, the specific skill implications
of delegating work to agentic AI in software development remain understudied.
5.5.2. Skill Transformation Under Agentic AI and the Automation–Augmentation Paradox
The literature consistently shows that AI affects novices and experts in different ways. Novices
gain more immediate benefits from AI assistance, while experienced developers tend to use AI
more selectively and focus on oversight (Lu et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025; Sowa et al., 2021). Yet
existing research does not compare how skills evolve for these two groups when working with
agentic AI. The experiential differences highlighted in automation and augmentation literature
(Raisch & Krakowski, 2021) remain largely conceptual and untested in real software
development settings.
5.5.3. Incomplete Understanding of Differences Between Freshers and Experienced
Developers
The existing studies show strong indications that AI affects junior and senior workers differently
(e.g., larger productivity boosts for novices; experts shifting toward orchestration and
oversight), but to the best of our knowledge, very little or no study explicitly compares freshers
vs. experienced software professionals in terms of skill development, upskilling, or deskilling
from human-agentic AI collaboration.
5.6. Future Research
Based on the gaps found, our research will examine the impact of human–agentic AI
collaboration on the developer skills in the context of software development. A key unresolved
issue is whether using AI in software development helps people truly learn new skills or instead
makes them dependent on the technology. Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) found that AI can help
beginners perform at a level closer to experienced workers by transferring expert knowledge.
However, it is still unclear whether users retain these skills over time or whether the learning
disappears once the AI support is removed.
AI-assisted coding shows faster task completion and higher output quality (as suggested by
recent working papers discussed in Section 5.2). However, these studies rarely examine how
HAIC influences technical, interpersonal, and conceptual skills. Performance improvements do
not automatically translate into deeper learning or genuine skill development. This requires
systematic investigation of skill transformation, not only productivity gains.
This research further attempts to understand how using agentic AI in software development
affects the skills that developers build at different experience levels. This includes examining
whether entry-level developers still learn basic programming skills when AI handles routine
tasks, and whether experienced developers use AI to solve more complex problems or mainly
to work faster on their usual tasks.

Implementing this research agenda requires interdisciplinary collaboration bridging computer
science, organizational behavior, cognitive psychology, and learning sciences. As Hoffman et
al. (2023) noted regarding human-AI teaming measurement, developing "explanation goodness,
user satisfaction, mental models, curiosity, trust, and human-AI performance" metrics demands
theoretical integration across traditionally separate domains.
Our research will employ a mixed-methods approach combining quantitative and qualitative
data collection and analysis. Mixed methods are appropriate because the research questions
span multiple levels, from measuring skill development outcomes (suited to quantitative
methods) to understanding collaboration processes and human-AI augmentation strategies
(suited to qualitative methods).
The research will target approximately 150-200 developers total, with roughly equal numbers
of freshers (0-2 years of experience) and experienced developers (3+ years of experience). This
sample size for quantitative analysis will be 10-15 developers and will be a subset from all
participants. Developers will be recruited through participating organizations, professional
networks, and online developer communities.
